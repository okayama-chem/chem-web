### Unveiling the Shapes of Matter: What is a Crystal Structure?

All matter around us is made up of tiny atoms and molecules. The way these atoms and molecules arrange themselves is called a "crystal structure." This shape is incredibly important because it determines a substance's properties, such as its hardness, color, or whether it conducts electricity. Knowing the invisible arrangement of molecules is the essential first step in developing new medicines and materials.

### Revealing Shapes Through Experimentation

So, how do scientists uncover these invisible molecular arrangements? One powerful method is "crystal structure determination," an experimental technique. This involves shining X-rays or other forms of light onto a substance and analyzing how the light scatters. By doing so, researchers can pinpoint the exact positions of atoms and molecules. In this research, scientists utilized this experimental approach to reveal the crystal structure of a particular substance and obtained detailed data. This data typically includes the precise 3D coordinates of atoms, the size and angles of the crystal's basic unit (cell parameters), and the symmetry of the molecular arrangement (space group).

### A Global Database for Molecular Shapes

The crystal structure data obtained in this study has been registered in the "Cambridge Structural Database (CSD)." The CSD is an international repository, much like an encyclopedia of molecular shapes, compiling millions of experimentally determined crystal structures discovered by researchers worldwide. By registering new data in the CSD, other scientists can freely access and utilize this information, leading to further scientific discoveries and technological advancements. This paper contributes to the scientific community by providing experimentally determined crystal structure data to this vital database.

### Why is Knowing the "Shape" So Important?

Understanding the "shape" of matter is fundamental to chemistry and directly impacts practical applications. For instance, to comprehend how a drug binds to a disease-causing protein, it's crucial to know the precise shapes of both the drug and the protein. Similarly, developing higher-performance electronic materials or eco-friendly catalysts requires an accurate understanding of how atoms and molecules are arranged. Thus, experimentally determining the true "shape" of matter and sharing it globally is an indispensable activity for building future science and technology.
