### The Crucial Role of CO2 Transport and Storage for Climate Change
One of the key strategies to combat global warming is Carbon Capture and Storage (CCS), a technology that captures carbon dioxide (CO2) emissions from industrial sources and stores them deep underground. When large quantities of CO2 are transported, they are typically moved as a fluid (gas or liquid) through pipelines. However, even a small amount of "water" mixed with the CO2 can lead to unexpected problems.

### Water: A Source of Troubles – Hydrates and Corrosion
Water present in CO2 can, under specific low-temperature and high-pressure conditions, combine with CO2 molecules to form "hydrates"—ice-like solid compounds. These hydrates can block pipelines. Additionally, water can cause "corrosion," which is the degradation of the metal pipes. To prevent these issues and ensure safe and efficient CO2 transport and storage, it is crucial to accurately understand "how much water dissolves in CO2 (solubility)."

### Deciphering Water Solubility from a Molecular Viewpoint
Our research employed "molecular simulation," a computational method, to investigate water solubility in CO2 at a detailed molecular level. Specifically, we predicted water solubility by calculating "intermolecular interactions" (the attractive or repulsive forces between molecules) between water and CO2 molecules and evaluating their "chemical potential" (an indicator of a substance's tendency to reach a stable state). These calculations were performed across a wide range of temperatures and pressures relevant to actual CO2 transport and storage.

### Unraveling an Apparently Strange Phenomenon!
The study revealed that the formation of hydrates reduces water solubility in CO2. This occurs because water molecules are locked into the hydrate structure, decreasing the amount of free-moving water in the CO2 fluid.
Interestingly, we observed an apparently strange phenomenon: as CO2 was compressed, water solubility (when expressed in "mole fraction" units) sometimes "decreased" under certain conditions. However, when we examined solubility using "molarity" (number of molecules per unit volume), this peculiar behavior disappeared, revealing a more natural trend. We demonstrated that evaluating solubility in terms of molarity is highly effective for understanding intermolecular interactions and is also beneficial for correlating solubility with water-induced corrosion.

### Contributing to Safe CO2 Transport and Storage
The deep molecular-level insights gained from this research significantly contribute to the development of technologies aimed at preventing CO2 pipeline blockages (due to hydrate formation) and mitigating water-induced corrosion. Solving complex societal challenges by theoretically unraveling the behavior of molecules is precisely what makes theoretical chemistry so fascinating and appealing.
