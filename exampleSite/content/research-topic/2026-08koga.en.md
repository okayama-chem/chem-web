### Hidden "Two-Dimensional Water" in a Mineral

We usually encounter water as a liquid or as solid "ice." While many types of ice are known, there are also unique collections of water molecules found within minerals, existing in special environments.
The mineral "martyite (Zn₃(V₂O₇)(OH)₂・2H₂O)" has a porous framework with a honeycomb-like structure, where water molecules (H₂O) are confined. This layer of water molecules behaves like "two-dimensional ice," but at room temperature, they are dynamically disordered, meaning they move randomly without a fixed crystal structure.

### What is "Ferroaxial Order" in Disordered Water Molecules?

This research revealed that the water molecules in martyite, previously thought to be randomly moving, actually exhibit a special kind of "order."
Although the water molecules are randomly arranged at room temperature, under specific conditions, six water molecules connect in a ring, forming a toroidal (donut-shaped) H₂O hexamer through "hydrogen bonds" (weak bonds connecting water molecules). The phenomenon where these donut-shaped groups align in a specific direction is called "ferroaxial order." This is analogous to ferromagnetism, where magnetic poles align, but here it's an alignment of an "axis" rather than magnetic moments.

### Why is the Order of Water Molecules in Minerals Important?

The discovery of this "ferroaxial order" suggests that martyite is an ideal model system for studying "two-dimensional ice."
Water molecules are known to form various types of ice (polymorphs) depending on temperature and pressure. Beyond the ice we see everyday, it is believed that many unknown and fascinating forms of ice exist deep within the Earth and in outer space.
This study provides a clue to understanding how water molecules behave and form new orders in the unique environment of minerals. Ultimately, it may contribute to unraveling the mystery of the diverse crystal structures (polymorphism) that water can adopt.
