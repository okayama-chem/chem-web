### Solar Water Splitting: The Challenge of Charge Separation in Photocatalysts

As solutions to global warming and energy issues, technologies for "water splitting" – using solar energy to decompose water into hydrogen and oxygen – are attracting significant attention. Substances that promote this reaction are called "photocatalysts". Layered photocatalysts, in particular, are highly promising due to their visible-light absorption and suitable band-edge positions for water decomposition.

However, layered photocatalysts face a major challenge. When exposed to light, they generate electrons and holes (electron vacancies), but these charge carriers predominantly migrate in-plane along the layered frameworks. This makes it difficult to spatially separate the sites for reduction (electron-accepting reaction) and oxidation (electron-donating reaction), leading to low efficiency. Especially in plate-like photocatalyst particles, the lateral distances for charge carrier migration are long, increasing the likelihood of recombination and loss.

### Redirecting Charge Flow with Spiral Growth and Nanosteps

To overcome this limitation, we developed an innovative method: introducing "step structures" onto the surface of layered photocatalysts. These step structures shorten the long lateral migration distance of charge carriers and spatially separate the redox sites, significantly enhancing charge separation efficiency.

Using the layered oxyhalide "Bi₄NbO₈Cl" as a model photocatalyst, we successfully formed nanoscale steps (nanosteps) on the basal planes of plate-like crystals. This was achieved by utilizing a special crystal growth mechanism called "screw-dislocation-driven spiral growth" during "flux synthesis" (a method where crystals are grown using a low-melting salt as a solvent).

### Surface Engineering Boosts Hydrogen Evolution Rate 16-Fold!

Furthermore, we applied "acid etching" – a chemical surface treatment – to the photocatalysts with nanosteps. This process converted the basal planes, which were originally electron-rich, into hole-accumulating surfaces. This change was confirmed by "surface photovoltage spectroscopy," a technique that measures light-induced potential changes on material surfaces.

As a result, it became possible to preferentially deposit reduction "cocatalysts" (substances that assist the main catalyst) onto the electron-rich nanosteps. This asymmetric cocatalyst loading promoted anisotropic (directional) charge separation, leading to a remarkable 16-fold enhancement in the photocatalytic hydrogen evolution rate compared to the untreated sample. This represents one of the highest "apparent quantum efficiencies" reported for layered oxyhalide photocatalysts.

Moreover, the etched samples with nanosteps also demonstrated enhanced photocatalytic water oxidation under visible-light "Z-scheme water splitting" – a complex reaction involving two types of photocatalysts to split water into hydrogen and oxygen using solar energy.

### A New Strategy for Solar Energy Conversion

This study establishes a new design strategy for controlling carrier flow in layered photocatalysts: spiral-growth-induced step formation combined with surface etching. This approach unlocks the untapped potential of layered photocatalysts and is expected to significantly contribute to the advancement of solar energy conversion technologies.
