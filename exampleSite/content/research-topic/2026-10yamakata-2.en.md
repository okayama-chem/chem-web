### Turning CO2 into Valuable Chemicals: The Challenge of Photocatalysis

Converting carbon dioxide (CO2), a major contributor to global warming, into useful chemicals is a crucial goal for achieving a sustainable society. Among various approaches, "photocatalysis" stands out as a promising technology that uses solar energy to reduce (chemically transform) CO2.

However, a significant challenge in this field is achieving efficient "interfacial electron transfer" – the smooth exchange of electrons – between light-harvesting semiconductors and the "molecular catalysts" that actually convert CO2. This challenge is particularly acute for "narrow-bandgap semiconductors" (materials that can absorb a wider range of visible light, meaning they can react even with weaker light), because the thermodynamic driving force for electron transfer is often reduced. Traditionally, many studies have relied on metallic electron-transfer mediators like silver (Ag) nanoparticles to bridge this gap.

### A Breakthrough Material: K2LaTa2O6N (KLTON)

In this study, we focused on a new semiconductor material: K2LaTa2O6N (KLTON), a layered perovskite oxynitride (an oxide containing both oxygen and nitrogen). Despite its relatively narrow bandgap of 2.1 eV, we discovered that KLTON can serve as an exceptionally effective support for molecular catalysts, enabling highly efficient CO2 photoreduction *without* heavily relying on Ag nanoparticles.

We constructed a hybrid system using KLTON as the semiconductor support for a "supramolecular RuRu′ CO2-reduction catalyst" (a catalyst made of multiple ruthenium-containing molecules joined together). This hybrid selectively converted CO2 to formate (a valuable chemical produced from CO2) under visible-light irradiation, demonstrating over 99% selectivity, a high turnover number (TON) of 691 (indicating how many times the catalyst reacted), and an apparent quantum yield (AQY) of 6.1 ± 0.6% at 420 nm (a measure of efficiency based on absorbed photons).

Notably, while loading a small amount of Ag did increase the TON to 1077, this 1.5-fold enhancement was modest compared to the much larger Ag-induced activity enhancements reported for several conventional oxynitride-based hybrids. This highlights the remarkably weak Ag dependence of the KLTON-based system, underscoring its unique properties.

### Unraveling the Mechanism: Favorable Carrier Dynamics

So, how does KLTON achieve such efficient CO2 photoreduction without relying heavily on silver? To uncover the secret, we employed a variety of advanced analytical techniques.

Firstly, spatial photodeposition experiments using Ag and MnOx revealed that the terraced layered structure of KLTON exhibits spatially differentiated surface redox reactivity, meaning electron-accepting and electron-donating reactions occur at different locations on its surface.

Furthermore, we conducted experiments to track the movement of electrons (known as "carrier dynamics": how electrons are generated, where and how fast they move inside a material when light hits it) immediately after light absorption, using techniques like "time-resolved microwave conductivity (TRMC)" and "transient infrared absorption (TRIR) measurements." These revealed that KLTON possesses "favorable photogenerated-carrier dynamics," meaning the electrons and "holes" (electron vacancies) generated by light move in a way that promotes catalytic reactions. We also found that Ag loading subtly modifies these electron dynamics.

Additionally, "time-resolved emission measurements" suggested an interfacial electronic interaction between the Ru(II) photosensitizer and the KLTON surface. These results collectively demonstrate that KLTON's unique electronic behavior is key to its high CO2 reduction efficiency.

### Promising Future for Hybrid Photocatalysis

This research identifies favorable carrier dynamics and weak Ag dependence as key features of the KLTON-based molecular hybrid system. These findings highlight layered narrow-bandgap mixed-anion semiconductors (semiconductors containing multiple types of anions) as highly promising support materials for visible-light-driven molecular photocatalysis, such as CO2 reduction.

The ability to convert CO2 into valuable chemical resources using light energy is increasingly vital for addressing global warming and achieving a sustainable society.
