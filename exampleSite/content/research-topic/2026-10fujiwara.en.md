### Cutting-Edge Technology for In Vivo Imaging: Quantum Sensors and Nanodiamonds

In the fields of medicine and life sciences, techniques for observing the inside of living cells and tissues in detail are crucial. Recently, "quantum sensors" using tiny diamond particles (nanodiamonds) have attracted significant attention. Some nanodiamonds possess a special structure called a "nitrogen-vacancy (NV) center," which is a pair of a nitrogen atom and a missing atom (vacancy). By utilizing the "spin" property of electrons within these NV centers, it becomes possible to detect subtle changes in cells, such as temperature or magnetic fields, with extremely high sensitivity.

However, simply introducing nanodiamonds into a living body presents challenges: they may not be effectively taken up by cells, may not remain in the desired location, or might bind to unwanted substances. To address this, attaching a polymer called "polyglycerol (PG)" to the surface of nanodiamonds helps them remain stable in vivo and suppresses unwanted binding (nonspecific biomolecular adsorption). Yet, this PG grafting introduced a new problem: it made it difficult for nanodiamonds to be retained for long periods in specific organs like the intestine.

### Surface Charge Determines In Vivo Fate

This research focused on how the "charge" of the nanodiamond surface critically influences its retention in the intestine. The research team synthesized nanodiamonds grafted with polyglycerol, but with different types of terminal groups (chemical structures at the end of the molecule). Specifically, they created nanodiamonds with negatively charged -COOH and OSO3Na groups, and positively charged -NH2 groups. They then meticulously investigated how these charge differences affected the nanodiamonds' behavior (biocompatibility) within the model organism, *Caenorhabditis elegans* (a small nematode worm).

### Positive Charge Enhances Retention

The experimental results revealed a remarkable finding: positively charged nanodiamonds (ND-PG-NH2) exhibited the highest intestinal retention in *C. elegans*. Furthermore, these nanodiamonds showed no detectable toxicity to the worms, maintained bright fluorescence, and preserved stable NV spin signals, which are crucial for quantum sensing. By combining this labeling strategy with an "adaptive masking algorithm"—an image processing technique that automatically removes unwanted halo artifacts from images—they successfully achieved reliable two-dimensional spin-active fluorescence imaging (ODMR imaging) across the entire worm body.

### Advancing Life Sciences with Quantum Sensors

This study demonstrates that precise engineering of the surface charge of nanodiamond quantum sensors is a highly practical strategy for efficient bioimaging in living systems. These findings significantly broaden the potential applications of nanodiamond-based quantum sensors in various fields, such as early disease diagnosis and treatment, and elucidating the mechanisms of life phenomena. The power of chemistry will continue to advance technologies that allow us to "see" into the microscopic world of life.
