### What is a "Solid-Liquid Interface"?

Many places in our daily lives involve a "solid-liquid interface"—where a solid meets a liquid. For example, the electrodes in batteries, sensors working in aqueous solutions, or even cell membranes in our bodies are all types of solid-liquid interfaces. At these interfaces, electrical forces (called "electrochemical potentials") drive various chemical reactions and energy conversion processes. However, until now, it was unclear how deeply this electrical influence extends into the solid material.

### Discovering a "Universal Law" for Electrochemical Information Transfer!

This study investigated how electrochemical information, exchanged at the solid-liquid interface, propagates into the solid. We discovered that the transfer of this information is universally controlled by a single "dimensionless factor, $u$." Simply put, this factor $u$ is determined by the ratio of the "effective electrostatic separation between the liquid and the probe location" to the "electrostatic propagation length in the solid" (how far electricity can travel in the solid).
The research demonstrated that this information transfer follows a universal inverse-square scaling law—meaning the signal weakens with the square of the distance—independent of the microscopic details of the probe used. This is similar to how the brightness of light diminishes as you move further away from its source.

### Towards New Design Principles for "Electrochemical Sensing"

This universal law reveals that "electrostatic screening" by the solid fundamentally limits information transfer across solid-liquid interfaces. By understanding this "attenuation law," we can derive quantitative design principles for "subsurface electrochemical sensing"—the challenging task of detecting substances located slightly beneath the surface of a material.
This discovery is expected to contribute to the development of higher-performance sensors and advance new energy conversion technologies.
